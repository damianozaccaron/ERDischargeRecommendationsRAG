MALE, 47 YEARS OLD
Medical history:
- Pathological proximate medical history: The patient presented with swelling, redness and warmth in his left leg, which had developed approximately 5 days previously and had been progressively worsening, accompanied by a high fever (maximum 39.4°C at home) and chills. He also reports marked weakness and loss of appetite. He denies having a cough, shortness of breath or chest pain. He does not report any recent trauma to the leg.
- Remote pathology: The patient is homeless. He reports active intravenous drug use (heroin). No formally diagnosed medical conditions are known. He is not on any regular home treatment. No declared drug allergies are known. 

Objective examination: The patient is alert, oriented but uncooperative. General condition is poor. Markedly dehydrated and malnourished. Personal hygiene is severely neglected. Vesicular breath sounds are present in all lung fields; no additional sounds. Cardiac sounds: tachycardic, rhythmic. On cardiac auscultation, a 3/6 holosystolic murmur is heard at the mid-precordium, radiating towards the axilla; this has not been reported in the patient’s Medical history nor documented in previous admissions. Abdomen soft to touch, not tender. Negative for CVA Tenderness and Blumberg’s signs. Left lower limb: erythematous patch with sharp, irregular margins extending from the ankle to the middle third of the leg; skin warm, oedematous and tender on palpation. Multiple venepuncture scars are present along the veins of both forearms and the left popliteal fossa. No fluctuation suggestive of a superficial abscess. No contralateral dependent oedema. Blood pressure 98/62 mmHg. Heart Rate 118 bpm. SpO₂ 96% on oxygen. Body Temperature 39.1°C.

Clinical diary:
• Venous access established with difficulty due to compromised venous access; two blood cultures taken from separate sites prior to any antibiotic therapy. Biochemistry, complete blood count, inflammatory markers, procalcitonin, coagulation profile, renal and hepatic function tests, and serological tests for HIV, HBV and HCV were carried out.
• ECG performed: sinus tachycardia, Heart Rate 118 bpm, no ST-segment changes, no signs of conduction disturbances.
• Arterial blood gas analysis was performed: mild hypoxaemia (PaO₂ 68 mmHg), compensatory respiratory alkalosis, lactate 2.8 mmol/L (slightly elevated).
• A bolus infusion of 500 cc of normal saline is initiated for relative hypotension, resulting in an improvement in blood pressure to 108/68 mmHg. Continuous monitoring of vital signs is commenced.
• Laboratory results were reviewed: marked neutrophilic leucocytosis (WBC 22,400/μL, neutrophils 89%). C-reactive protein 28.4 mg/dL. Procalcitonin 18.2 ng/mL (normal range <0.5), markedly elevated, suggestive of a systemic bacterial infection. Creatinine 1.6 mg/dL (mild renal impairment). AST/ALT slightly elevated. Coagulation: PT and aPTT at the upper limits of normal. HIV negative, HCV positive, HBsAg negative.
• A bedside echocardiogram was performed: no pericardial effusion. No diffuse B-lines. Ventricular systolic function visually preserved. On examination of the right heart, a mobile hyperechoic image was observed at the level of the tricuspid valve, suggestive of a vegetation. No significant pleural effusion.
• Given the overall clinical picture — erysipelas/cellulitis in an intravenous drug user, high fever, markedly elevated procalcitonin, elevated lactate levels, a newly detected heart murmur and suspicious echocardiographic findings — the picture is strongly suggestive of infective endocarditis with a likely cutaneous or venipuncture entry site. The infectious disease specialist on call is contacted urgently. 
• Broad-spectrum empirical antibiotic therapy is initiated following the collection of blood cultures: vancomycin 25 mg/kg by slow infusion (estimated weight 65 kg, dose 1,625 mg rounded to 1,500 mg) to cover methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), a priority pathogen in intravenous drug users.
• The infectious disease specialist assesses the patient in A&E and confirms the need for urgent hospitalisation for a dedicated transthoracic/transoesophageal echocardiogram, monitoring of blood cultures and continuation of targeted antibiotic therapy.

Diagnosis: erysipelas/cellulitis of the left lower limb — suspected infective endocarditis (likely involving the tricuspid valve) in an IDU
Prognosis: urgent hospitalisation

). 5.3.1. Blood culture-positive infective endocarditis Positive blood cultures remain the cornerstone of IE diagnosis and provide live bacteria for both identification and susceptibility testing. At least three sets of blood cultures should be obtained at 30-minute intervals prior to antibiotic therapy, each containing 10 mL of blood, and should be incubated in both aerobic and anaerobic atmospheres.Sampling should be obtained from a peripheral vein rather than from a central venous catheter (because of the risk of contamination and misleading interpretation), using a meticulous sterile technique. In the absence of previous antimicrobial therapy, this is virtually always sufficient to identify the usual causative microorganisms. The need for culture before antibiotic administration is self-evident. In IE, bacteraemia is almost constant and has two implications: (i) there is no rationale for delaying blood sampling to coincide with peaks of fever; and (ii) nearly all blood cultures are positive during bacteraemia. As a result, a single positive blood culture should be regarded cautiously for establishing IE diagnosis.

Rationale The development of IE usually requires several conditions, including the presence of predisposing risk factors (i.e. a surface/structure that could be colonized by bacteria), pathogens entering the bloodstream, and the competence of the host’s immune response.
