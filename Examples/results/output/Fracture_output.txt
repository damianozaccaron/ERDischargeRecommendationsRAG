In complete injuries, the aim of re-alignment is correction of severe distraction defect rather than stricture prevention [262, 276, 284]. Re-alignment can be done by either open or endoscopic techniques [284, 285]. The open technique is associated with longer operation times, greater blood loss and longer hospital stays. As such, endoscopic re-alignment is now preferred [264]. Using a flexible/rigid cystoscope and biplanar fluoroscopy, a guidewire is placed inside the bladder under direct visual control, over this, a catheter is placed. If necessary, two cystoscopes can be used: one retrograde (per urethra) and one antegrade (suprapubic route through the bladder neck) [241]. The duration of catheterisation is three weeks for partial and six weeks for complete ruptures with voiding urethrography upon catheter removal [241]. It is important to avoid traction on the balloon catheter as it can damage the remaining sphincter mechanism at the bladder neck [241]. Data from an RCT demonstrated no difference in the urethral stricture rate following early re-alignment for complete rupture PFUI [276].

TASK:
Produce discharge recommendations in two sections, do not add anything else.

SECTION 1 — DISCHARGE RECOMMENDATIONS
A numbered list of concise instructions for the patient.
Rules:
- One or two sentences per item. Be direct: "Continue...", "Stop...", "A prescription is issued for...".
- Combine closely related actions into a single item (e.g. stopping a medication and adopting an alternative is one recommendation, not two). Never combine medication prescriptions with non-medication actions.
- For medications: always include drug name, dose, route, and frequency (e.g. "every 12 hours"), but do not make up information. Take dosing from the guideline excerpts or tables when they state it; otherwise copy the exact dosing from the clinical note. If neither states the frequency, write "as directed by the specialist".
- Dosing may be taken from a cited table; copy the value or range exactly as written, never compute or interpolate.
- Where a table distinguishes routes, use the oral maintenance column for home discharge and ignore intravenous columns.
- Before prescribing a drug that appears in a table, check every contraindication or criteria column against the clinical note; if a contraindication is present, do not prescribe and state this in Section 2.
- If a table gives a range rather than a single dose, write the range.
- For lifestyle factors (smoking, alcohol, diet, exercise): use strong advisory tone ("strongly recommended", "strongly advised") rather than direct orders.
- For lifestyle factors, if the patient is already doing the right thing (e.g. non-smoking, not overweight),don't acknowledge it. Only give instructions for things that need to be changed or started.
- For follow-up appointments and specialist referrals, use the phrasing: "A prescription is issued for [specialist type] evaluation for [clinical purpose]" and always specify the specialist type from the clinical note (e.g. "angiologist", "cardiologist", "internist") and the clinical purpose of the visit (e.g. "for thrombophilia screening", "for initiation of anticoagulant therapy", "for echocardiographic follow-up").
- Do NOT explain why. No rationale, no "because...", no "to reduce the risk of...", no pathophysiology.
- Do NOT speculate about future treatment decisions (duration of therapy, drug switches). - No sub-bullets, no bold, no markdown headers, no category groupings. Just a numbered list.
- Do NOT cite [E#] in this section. Citations belong only in Section 2.
- The second-to-last item must always be: "Go to the nearest emergency department immediately if you experience [relevant warning symptoms for this diagnosis]."
- The last item must always be: "You are referred back to your general practitioner for any further diagnostic or therapeutic needs and ongoing care."
- If a topic cannot be addressed by the excerpts: "Refer to specialist for [topic]."
